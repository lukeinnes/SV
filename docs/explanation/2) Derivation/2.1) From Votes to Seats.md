# From Votes to Seats

Strengthened Voting (SV) proposes that legislative representation should not only recognise how many voters support a political party, but also the achievement of bringing those voters behind a common political programme. This Derivation develops that principle into a mathematical model, establishes a value for its central parameter, and explains how the model can be implemented as an electoral system.

The mathematical conclusions in this Derivation follow from the premises established throughout. These premises are political and normative judgements, open to criticism and alternative interpretations, as explored in the Evaluation.

Every electoral system must translate votes into legislative seats. The distinction between systems lies in the processes connecting these two quantities. We begin by examining two familiar approaches before introducing the additional step proposed by SV.

## First-Past-the-Post (FPTP)

In First-Past-the-Post (FPTP), each constituency elects a single representative. There is a **winner-takes-all** dynamic within each constituency, where the candidate receiving the most votes wins the seat irrespective of whether they receive an absolute majority.

Let $v_{i,c}$ represent the Raw Vote count received by Party $i$ in constituency $c$, and let $s_i$ represent the total number of seats won by that party. The process can be summarised as:

$$
v_{i,c}
\xrightarrow{\text{Constituency plurality and tally}}
s_i
$$

The geographical distribution of votes is therefore essential: two elections with identical national Vote Shares may produce substantially different distributions of seats.

## Proportional Representation (PR)

Under direct party-list Proportional Representation (PR), parties are allocated seats in proportion to their electoral support, subject to the practical rules of the system.

Let $v_i$ represent the votes received by Party $i$. Its **Vote Share**, $x_i$, is its proportion of the relevant vote total. In an election without an Electoral Threshold:

$$
x_i=\frac{v_i}{\sum_j v_j}
$$

Vote Share is expressed between 0 and 1, i.e. between 0% and 100%. The Vote Shares of all participating parties sum to 1.

In a legislature containing $S$ seats, a directly proportional allocation would entitle Party $i$ to $Sx_i$ seats. For example, a party winning 37.5% of the vote in a 460-seat legislature would be entitled to:

$$
460\times0.375=172.5\text{ seats}
$$

Since seats are indivisible, an **apportionment method** is needed to convert these proportional entitlements into whole numbers.

Many PR systems implement an **Electoral Threshold**, $\tau$, representing the minimum electoral support required for a party to qualify for seats. Parties falling below this threshold are excluded, and the votes of qualifying parties are normalised so that their Vote Shares still sum to 100%.

More precisely, let $V=\sum_j v_j$ denote the total valid Raw Votes, and let $\mathcal E$ be the set of parties meeting the threshold:

$$
\mathcal E={i:v_i\geq\tau V}
$$

The Vote Share of each qualifying party is then:

$$
\boxed{x_i=\frac{v_i}{\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal E}v_j}}
\qquad (i\in\mathcal E)
$$

For example, if parties collectively receiving 10% of the original vote fail the threshold, the remaining 90% is renormalised to 100%. A qualifying party originally receiving 30% of all valid votes would therefore have a Vote Share of $30/90=33\tfrac13%$ for seat allocation.

The general process of PR can therefore be summarised as:

$$
v_i\xrightarrow{\text{Threshold \& normalisation}}x_i
\xrightarrow{\text{Apportionment}}s_i
$$

## Strengthened Voting (SV)

Strengthened Voting retiains the distinction between raw Vote count and Vote Share, but introduces an additional quantity: **Political Mandate**, $M_i$.

Rather than allocating seats directly in proportion to Vote Share, SV calculates each party's Political Mandate as a funciton of its Vote Share. This Mandate recognises the achievement of bringing broader electoral support behind a common political programme.

Each party's proportion of the combined Political Mandate is called its **Mandate Share**, $f_i$. This represents its intended proportion of legislative representation before the allocation of whole seats.

As with PR, an apportionment method converts the intended distribution into the actual number of seats, $s_i$.

The complete SV process can therefore be summarised as:

$$
v_i
\xrightarrow{\text{Threshold \& normalisation}}
x_i
\xrightarrow{\text{Mandate Equation}}
M_i
\xrightarrow{\text{Mandate normalisation}}
f_i
\xrightarrow{\text{Apportionment}}
s_i
$$

The defining distinction is the introduction of Political Mandate between Vote Share and the intended distribution of legislative seats.
